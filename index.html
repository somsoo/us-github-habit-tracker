📖 Behavioral Science: Habit Loops and Visual Streaks

Visual habit tracking capitalizes on foundational principles of behavioral psychology and neurobiology documented in contemporary cognitive science:

  • The "Don't Break the Chain" Principle: Popularized by comedian Jerry Seinfeld, visualizing an uninterrupted sequence of completed days leverages loss aversion; breaking a hard-won streak creates immediate psychological friction against procrastination.
  • Immediate Reward Feedback: In accordance with James Clear's Atomic Habits, marking a day completed provides an instantaneous dopamine reward, reinforcing the basal ganglia habit loop (Cue → Craving → Response → Reward).
  • Automaticity Timelines: Landmark research by Dr. Phillippa Lally at University College London demonstrates that habit automaticity requires an average of 66 consecutive daily repetitions before becoming an effortless routine.

Scientific references: Lally P. et al. (2010) How are habits formed, Eur. J. Soc. Psychol.; Clear J. (2018) Atomic Habits; Wood W. & Neal D. (2009) The habitual consumer.

❓ Frequently Asked Questions

How does the contribution heatmap track streaks?

Every day you complete your habit, the corresponding square lights up in green. The system automatically computes your current consecutive unbroken streak and your all-time longest streak.

Can I click past days to backfill or adjust records?

Yes. Clicking or tapping any square on the 52-week grid toggles its completion state through intensity levels (0 to 4), allowing you to accurately backfill missed logs.

Can I track multiple different habits?

Yes. You can switch between built-in habits such as coding, reading, and workouts, or add unlimited custom habits with dedicated color palettes and individual contribution grids.

Is my habit data private and stored securely?

Yes. All habit grids, streaks, and timestamps are saved solely in your local web browser storage. No account creation is required and no habit data is transmitted to external servers.

⚠️ Note on Habit Consistency

Never miss twice. Missing a single day due to travel or illness is an anomaly; missing two consecutive days is the start of a new, contrary habit. If you miss a day, jump back in immediately the following morning.
